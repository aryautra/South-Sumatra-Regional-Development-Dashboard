South Sumatra Regional Development

Poverty, jobs, human development, inequality and output across 17 regencies and cities, 2015-2025.

Rank 1 = highest value. Click an area to select it.

Problem statement

Context

South Sumatra has 17 regencies and cities with very different economies. BPS publishes official statistics, but they sit in separate tables, one indicator at a time.

The problem

Planners and analysts cannot quickly compare areas, follow trends or see where progress is uneven, so gaps between areas are easy to miss.

Questions this answers

  • Where is poverty highest, and how fast is it falling?
  • Does a larger economy mean better human development?
  • Which indicators move together?

Data notes

Province GRDP is the sum of the 17 areas, and growth is available for areas only. A few extreme values (for example Empat Lawang 2020 and OKI 2022 growth) and the sharp 2025 drop in Gini are kept as published and flagged for checking against BPS.

Methodology

1

Define

Choose six indicators covering poverty, jobs, human development, inequality, output and growth.

2

Collect

Download BPS tables into an Excel template with fixed area codes and years, recording each source table.

3

Clean and check

Align units, areas and years, compute province GRDP as the sum of 17 areas, and flag unusual values.

4

Analyze

Compare and rank areas, measure change since 2015, and test relationships with Pearson correlation across 17 areas and 11 years.

5

Visualize

Build an interactive dashboard with region vs region trends, ranking, year zoom and highlight.

6

Publish

Host one self-contained HTML file on GitHub Pages so anyone can open it.

Outcome

In short

South Sumatra’s poverty fell from 14.25% to 10.15% between 2015 and 2025, but progress is uneven across the 17 areas. The dashboard turns scattered BPS tables into one place where any two areas can be compared in seconds, and it points to where attention may be needed first.

What the project delivers

  • A live, interactive dashboard: compare any two areas, zoom into any year range, highlight a year to read exact values, and switch the summary cards between the province and any area.
  • A ranking of all 17 areas for each indicator, with the change since 2015 and the position above or below the provincial figure.
  • An Excel data template with a fixed layout and source fields, so the figures can be refreshed when BPS publishes new data.
  • A documented method and README on GitHub, so anyone can follow and check how the numbers were built.

Who can use it, and for what

  • Regional planners and researchers: spot the areas with the highest poverty or unemployment and follow their trends.
  • Analysts and students: compare indicators across areas without opening separate BPS tables.
  • Employers and reviewers: see the full workflow, from raw data to a published tool.

These are intended uses, not measured impact.

What the findings suggest (to explore)

  • Poverty gaps remain: Muratara is still highest at 15.25% while Pagar Alam is lowest at 7.70%, so programs could target the highest-poverty areas first.
  • Areas with higher HDI tend to have lower poverty, so education and health are worth testing as levers.
  • Output is not welfare: Muara Enim and Musi Banyuasin rank 2 and 3 in GRDP but 7 and 10 in HDI, so poverty and HDI should be tracked next to GRDP.
  • Output is concentrated: three areas produce 62% of provincial GRDP, which raises the question of how growth reaches the other areas.
  • Urban unemployment stands out: Palembang has 6.81% against 3.69% for the province, so job matching in cities deserves a closer look.

Next steps

  • Add population to compute true per-capita GRDP, plus GRDP by industry and inflation.
  • Verify the flagged values (extreme growth figures and the 2025 Gini drop) against BPS.
  • Extend the same template to other provinces for comparison.
  • Test the relationships with regression, since correlation shows patterns, not causes.
